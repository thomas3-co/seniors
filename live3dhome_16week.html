Week 1 — Getting Started with Live 3D Home

2 class days · 1 period each

Day 1: The Interface, Walls & Rooms

Tour the workspace and toolbars, then draw your first walls and auto-detect rooms.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Plan View — The 2D top-down view used for drawing walls and rooms., 3D View — The live 3D preview of your design., Toolbar — Toolbox with wall, room, door, and furniture tools., Wall Tool — A structural element you draw to enclose space., Room Tool — Automatically labels an enclosed area and its square footage., Precise Dimensions — Locking measurements to exact values while drawing.

Every design starts in Plan View and comes to life in 3D View. Accurate wall placement now saves hours of rework later — always check dimensions before moving on.

Day 2: Doors, Windows & Practice

Place and customize openings, then practice on a small room shell.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Auto-Fit — Snapping an opening to sit exactly inside a wall., Swing Direction — Which way a door or casement opens., Sill Height — Height of the bottom of a window above the floor.

Doors and windows aren't just holes in a wall — swing direction and sill height affect real livability. Use the rest of the period to place openings in a practice room shell.

Week 2 — Building the Structure

2 class days · 1 period each

Day 1: Floors, Levels & Roof Design

Add a second story, connect it with stairs, then generate and customize a roof.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Level — A separate floor stacked above or below the current one., Stair Tool — Connects two levels and auto-calculates step count., Pitch — Steepness of a roof surface., Hip Roof — A roof with four sloped sides., Gable Roof — A roof with two sloped sides meeting at a ridge.

Multi-story homes require every level to line up. The auto-roof tool gets you 90% there — small pitch and overhang adjustments make the last 10% look intentional.

Furniture placement should follow how a room is actually used — walk the traffic path before you place a single chair. Materials do storytelling too: a warm wood floor changes the whole feel of a room.

Vocabulary

Week 5 — Advanced Roofs

2 class days · 1 period each

Day 1: Contour-Based Custom Roofs

Break out of AutoRoof presets by editing a roof's outline directly.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Contour Point — A draggable point along a roof outline used to reshape it into a custom, non-standard form., Custom Roof — A roof built from a hand-edited outline rather than the AutoRoof algorithm.

Contour points let you build shapes AutoRoof can't — at the cost of needing to keep the shape watertight yourself.

Day 2: Customizing Roofs & Dormers

Fine-tune roof planes and add a dormer for light and headroom.

Tap to flip
Vocabulary

Dormer — A small roofed structure projecting from a sloped roof, usually housing a window., Roof Plane — One flat sloped surface of a roof that can be edited independently.

Dormers add headroom and light to an attic level, but each one is a small roof intersection — get the base roof right first.

Day 1: A-Frame Houses

A-frames have almost no vertical exterior wall — most of what you'd normally model as "wall" is actually roof.

Split-levels and basement garages both hinge on getting Level heights right before you draw a single wall.

Read Me — Detailed Notes

Written lessons adapted from the official Live Home 3D (iOS & iPadOS) help documentation, with real screenshots from the app. Each section links to the full official page and can be downloaded on its own as a PDF. Opening a section counts toward your weekly reading check.

Introduction to Live Home 3D

Live Home 3D is an app for creating floor plans and 3D renderings of interiors such as offices and living rooms — it can also be used to design an entire house and its surrounding property. For interior design it supplies libraries of 3D furniture, materials, and light sources, including catalog items modeled after real manufacturers. It can also model a house's exterior and landscape. Finished work can be exported or printed as floor plans and 3D renders, and the app can export a 3D model, panoramas, and fly-through movies. There are two editions — Live Home 3D and Live Home 3D Pro — and this course covers features common to both.

  1. Open the app and explore the workspace: Plan View, 3D View, and the side toolbar.
  2. Familiarize yourself with essential panels: Project Tree, Inspector, Library.
  3. Learn the basic workflow: walls → rooms → openings → furniture → materials → lighting → render.
  4. Practice: draw a single rectangular room and watch it appear in 3D View instantly.
Analogy: Plan View is your blueprint; 3D View is the finished building standing in front of you — they update together in real time.
Tip: Save your project after every major step so you can always undo a big mistake.

The Workspace — The Main Window

The main window is made up of four parts: the toolbar at the top (with 2D Plan, 3D view, or 2D Elevation tools, depending on which view is active); the Library panel, which can show the 3D object library, the material library, or the Project Tree; the editing area itself, which displays your floor plan, elevation, or 3D view; and the Inspector, which shows properties for whatever is selected.

  1. Use the toolbar buttons to switch between the 2D Floor Plan and 3D views.
  2. Open or close the Library panel to browse objects, materials, or the Project Tree.
  3. Open the Inspector to see and edit the properties of anything you select.
Tip from the docs: On a narrow screen, the 2D/3D switch may appear as a single button — a long-press reveals all view options at once.

Read the full official guide →

Working with Objects — Basics

Objects fall into two broad groups: those created with drawing tools right inside the app (walls, floors, roofs), and pre-designed 3D models from the library or imported from elsewhere (furniture, doors, windows, staircases). To select an object, tap it in the 2D or 3D view; a selected object shows a green boundary in 3D, and resize/rotation handles in 2D plan view.

  1. Tap an object once to select it; tap Done in the toolbar to deselect.
  2. Drag a selected object to move it, or drag its round handle to rotate it.
  3. Long-press an object to open its context menu.
  4. On a crowded floor plan, tap up to three times in the same spot to drill down from room → group → individual object.
Tip: Lock an object in the Project Tree to stop it from being moved by accident while you work around it.

Read the full official guide →

Introduction to Roofs

A roof is its own object type, built inside the app from the outline of the external walls and a style you pick in the Roof Assistant (gable, hip, mansard, and others). A roof has several sides — gables count as sides too — and a side can be made of multiple segments. An AutoRoof automatically covers the entire top of the highest story and reshapes itself if you edit the walls below it; the Pro edition also supports custom roofs with a user-defined area. To add a skylight, simply install a window into the roof.

  1. Open the Roof Assistant and pick a base roof type.
  2. Adjust pitch and segments to fine-tune the shape.
  3. Drag a material from the library onto the roof in 3D view to change its finish.
Tip from the docs: You can reopen the Roof Assistant any time via the roof's context menu → "Change Roof Type."

Read the full official guide →

Working with Materials

Materials apply color to an object or map a texture onto its surface, and they also control how that surface reflects and scatters light. The built-in material library lives in the Library panel, and you can add your own materials to it. Whatever is currently applied to a selected object shows up in the Object Materials tab of the Inspector. Custom materials can be backed up or exported as a library archive — handy for restoring your library after reinstalling the app, moving to a new device, or sharing a matching material set with a classmate on the same project.

  1. Select a wall, floor, or object and open the Object Materials tab.
  2. Drag a material from the Library panel onto the surface.
  3. Export a library archive if you want to reuse your custom materials elsewhere.
Analogy: Applying a material is like wallpapering — get the scale right or the pattern reads as fake.

Read the full official guide →

Lighting — Light Sources

To make a 3D scene look realistic, the app lets you add and adjust light sources, and it can simulate light from the sun and moon as well as lamps and fire. Turning on reflections and shadows (the bulb icon in the 3D toolbar) makes a huge visual difference, at the cost of some rendering performance — the more light sources in a project, the longer a scene takes to render.

Without lights & shadows
With lights & shadows
  1. Set the sun/moon position using real geolocation, date, and time in the daylight settings.
  2. Drag a lamp into your project from the Lamps section of the object library.
  3. Adjust luminosity and color per light source in the Light Properties tab of the Inspector.
Tip: If the 3D view starts to lag, switch off a few individual lamps in the Inspector rather than all lighting at once.

Read the full official guide →

Cameras, Screenshots & Renders

A camera stores a location and view direction in 3D space, and the same set of cameras defines the views used for 3D renderings. Every project starts with five predefined cameras (front, back, two sides, and top) that can't be renamed, removed, or repositioned. You can add and adjust custom cameras of your own. A movie camera is different — it's a point along a movie track, a path you virtually travel while shooting a walkthrough video. Whichever camera is currently driving the 3D view is called the "active camera," and its position is shown on the floor plan as a frustum (a triangle showing direction and field of view).

  1. Select a camera in the Inspector, Project Tree, or floor plan to make it the active camera.
  2. Adjust its position or angle, then use it to generate a screenshot or high-resolution render.
  3. Set up a movie track if you want a moving walkthrough instead of a still render.

Advanced Roofs & House Forms (Weeks 5–6)

Once you're comfortable with AutoRoof, the next step is editing a roof's contour points directly to build shapes the assistant can't generate on its own — everything from an extra gable to an irregular, site-specific silhouette. Dormers extend that idea at a smaller scale, letting you cut usable, lit space out of an attic. The same "shell first" discipline applies to unusual house forms: an A-frame is a roof pretending to be walls, and a split-level or basement garage is really a Level-height problem before it's anything else.

  1. Open a roof's context menu and look for contour or point-editing options.
  2. Add a dormer once the base roof shape is finalized, not before.
  3. For A-frames and split-levels, sketch your Level heights on paper before touching the app.
Tip: Custom roof edits are easy to get wrong — keep a saved backup of the AutoRoof version before you start hand-editing.

Outdoor Spaces & Terrain (Weeks 7–8)

Balconies, porches, decks, and outdoor kitchens all use the same wall, room, and object tools you already know — the difference is weatherproof materials and an eye for how the space connects back to the interior. Terrain tools work at a larger scale: elevation points let you raise or lower the ground itself, and contour lines help you check the resulting slope at a glance. A courtyard sits between the two ideas — an exterior "room" carved out of the building's own footprint.

  1. Rough out your terrain slope before finalizing an outdoor deck or porch's height.
  2. Use contour lines to sanity-check that water would actually drain away from the house.
  3. Treat a courtyard's floor and walls with the same material care as any interior room.
Tip: Model the terrain around a sloped site before you finalize any outdoor structure's floor height.

Advanced Modeling & Materials (Weeks 9–10)

Boolean operations — union, subtract, and intersect — are the escape hatch for architectural details the object library doesn't already include: a custom alcove, a chimney chase, an irregular column. Multi-building projects apply the same organizational habits at a larger scale, keeping several structures and a shared site plan inside one file. On the surfacing side, a well-made tiled material depends on correct real-world scale as much as image quality, and a background image is often the fastest way to make an exterior render feel like it belongs to a real place.

  1. Use Boolean subtract to cut an opening a standard door or window tool can't make.
  2. Keep each building in a multi-house project named clearly in the Project Tree.
  3. Check a new tiled material's scale against a nearby door or person-height object.
Analogy: A Boolean operation is like using a cookie cutter — one shape stamps a hole or an addition into another.

Workflow, AR & Pro Features (Weeks 11–13)

As projects grow, workflow habits matter as much as modeling skill: trimming polygon count and light sources keeps a scene responsive, and a recent project archive or backup means a crash costs you minutes instead of days. Augmented reality tools flip the direction of that workflow — scanning a real room's point cloud gives you a rough floor plan to refine rather than build from scratch. Live Home 3D Pro and the app's real estate use cases both point toward the same audience: professionals who need construction-ready documentation or a listing render polished enough to market a property.

  1. Profile a slow project by turning off lights one at a time before assuming it's a hardware limit.
  2. Save a project archive before any AR scan import, in case the scan needs redoing.
  3. Compare a Pro-only feature you're curious about against the free-tier feature list before relying on it.
Tip: Treat an AR scan as a first draft floor plan — always verify a couple of key dimensions by hand.

Capstone Project Guide (Weeks 14–16)

Your capstone brings together every skill from the course into one original home design. Start with a short design brief and program — who the house is for and what rooms it needs — before opening the app. Build the shell first, furnish and material it second, then finish with lighting, a final render pass, and a walkthrough video. The last class day is a critique: presenting your design choices clearly and explaining your trade-offs is part of the assignment, not an afterthought.

  1. Write your one-paragraph design brief before Week 14, Day 1.
  2. Don't start furnishing until your shell (walls, levels, roof) is finished and saved.
  3. Reserve your highest-quality render settings for the very last pass, in Week 15–16.
  4. Prepare 2–3 sentences on the reasoning behind your biggest design decision for the final presentation.
Tip: Revisit the Read Me sections and screencasts from earlier weeks that match your specific design — you don't need to re-learn everything, just the parts your house actually uses.
